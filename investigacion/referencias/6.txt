Mâm Cơm
The family rice tray
mâm cơm
· /məm kəːm/ ·
“the tray of rice”
Vietnamese has no word for
entrée
. There is only
cơm
— rice — and everything laid around it. Every evening, in a hundred million homes, six or seven small
              dishes are set out on one round tray and eaten together, all at once, in any order. Not a sequence of
              courses. A conversation.

Three thousand years
on a circle
Việt Nam’s oldest surviving artefact is a bronze drum, cast in the
Đông Sơn
era: a perfect disc of concentric life — herons, boats, dancers, a fourteen-rayed sun at the centre. Its
                most ordinary object is a bamboo tray of exactly the same shape. Spin the drum. Every dish sits on a ray
                of the sun.

— “to eat rice” — simply means

Caramel-braised pork belly & eggs

Canh Chua Cá
Sour tamarind fish soup

Cá Kho Tộ
Clay-pot caramel fish

Một mâm đầy đủ · A full tray
This is dinner.
All of it, at once.
No starter, no main, no dessert. Everything arrives together and stays on the table until it is gone —
              which means a Vietnamese meal is not a schedule, it is a place you sit down in. Seven dishes, one tray,
              and no wrong order to eat them in.
7 dishes
1 tray
40 min cooking
20 min eating
2 hours still sitting there
Nguồn gốc · Origins
Con Rồng
Cháu Tiên
“Children of the Dragon, Grandchildren of the Fairy”
Lạc Long Quân
, a dragon lord of the sea, married
Âu Cơ
, a fairy of the mountains. She bore a sac of one hundred eggs, and from them one hundred sons. When the
                two finally parted, fifty followed their mother up into the highlands and fifty followed their father
                down to the coast.
That, Vietnamese children are told, is why we are the way we are: a people half water and half rock, who
                put the sea and the mountain on the same tray and eat them in the same mouthful. The eldest of those
                sons founded
Văn Lang
. His people cast bronze drums and flooded fields to grow rice.
Three thousand years later the drum is behind museum glass — and the rice is still on the table, still
                in the middle, still warm.
~1000 BCE · Văn Lang
Bronze casters at
Đông Sơn
pour the great drums while their neighbours flood the Red River delta to grow wet rice. Both
                  technologies survive.
The face of the drum
A fourteen-rayed sun at dead centre, ringed by bands of
Lạc
herons in flight, long boats, and dancers in feathered headdresses. A drum, a calendar, and a map of
                  an entire world — read from the middle outwards.
The tray
The same circle, in bamboo or red lacquer. Rice at the centre, dishes on the radius, nothing served in
                  courses, every bowl within every arm’s reach. Geometry as table manners.
Tonight
Two main dishes, a soup, a plate of greens, a saucer of pickles, one bowl of fish sauce in the middle.
                  Whoever gets home last still finds rice in the pot.
Năm mươi con theo cha xuống biển, năm mươi con theo mẹ lên non.
Fifty sons to the sea, fifty to the mountains.
Công thức · One of the seven
Thịt Kho Trứng
Caramel-braised pork belly and eggs · serves the whole tray
Seven dishes go on the tray, but only one of them is the dish a Vietnamese mother teaches you first. If
                you learn a single thing to cook from this page, learn this one.
It is the dish a household makes when it wants to stop cooking for a while — forgiving, better reheated
                than fresh, and the smell of the caramel going into the pot is, for a great many of us, the actual smell
                of home. One pot on a Sunday carries a family through most of the week.

Method
Marinate.
Toss the pork with the fish sauce, one spoon of sugar, the shallots, garlic, pepper and a pinch of
                    salt. Leave it thirty minutes at room temperature — longer in the fridge if you have the time.
Burn the sugar, carefully.
In a heavy pot over medium heat, melt two spoons of sugar dry, without stirring. Swirl the pan. When
                    it turns the colour of strong tea and smells faintly of toast, it is done. Ten seconds later it is
                    ruined.
Sear.
Tip in the pork and its marinade. It will hiss and seize. Turn the pieces until every side is
                    lacquered dark and the fat has begun to render.
Add the coconut water
and just enough plain water to come level with the meat. Bring it to a bare simmer and skim off the
                    grey foam that rises in the first few minutes.
Braise low, uncovered or barely covered,
for ninety minutes. Do not stir it — nudge it. The liquid should tremble, never roll.
Add the eggs
in the last thirty minutes so they take on colour without going rubbery. Roll them over once or
                    twice so they stain evenly.
Rest, then reheat.
It is good now and noticeably better tomorrow. Serve with a great deal of white rice, something
                    pickled, and something green.
On the caramel.
Nước màu
is the single skill this dish asks of you. Cook the sugar too little and the braise stays pale and
                sweet; too far and the whole pot turns bitter. Aim for the moment it stops smelling sweet and starts
                smelling toasted — and have the pork standing by, because there is no time to fetch it.
Nếp nhà · The way of the house
Six rules of the tray
A
mâm cơm
is a shared bowl, and shared bowls need etiquette. None of this is written down anywhere. All of it is
                taught before you can read.
Mời cơm
“Invite, then eat.”
The youngest at the table invites each elder to begin, by name and in order of age —
“Con mời bà ăn cơm.”
Nobody lifts chopsticks before that. It takes ten seconds and it sets the whole meal.
Ăn trông nồi, ngồi trông hướng
“Watch the pot as you eat; watch your seat as you sit.”
Take what you need while noticing what is left. Never take the last piece of anything without offering
                it first — and never twice in a row from the same plate.
Gắp cho người khác
“Serve someone else.”
The best piece in the dish does not go into your bowl. It goes into a grandparent’s, or a guest’s,
                placed there with your own chopsticks. Refusing it politely is part of the ritual too.
Cơm là chính
“Rice is the main thing.”
Every dish is seasoned to be eaten
with
rice, not alone. Taste the braise by itself and it will seem far too salty. That is not a mistake — it
                is the recipe working as designed.
Không cắm đũa
“Never stand your chopsticks up.”
Chopsticks pushed upright into a bowl of rice is an offering for the dead, set on an ancestral altar
                with incense. At a living table, lay them flat across the rim instead.
Ăn hết cơm
“Finish the rice.”
Leave a grain and someone will tell you about the person who grew it. Nobody teaches this one as a rule.
                Everybody enforces it.
Lời chào · The greeting
Ăn cơm chưa?
“Have you eaten rice yet?”
It is how Vietnamese people say hello — to a neighbour, on the phone, to a child coming through the door.
              It is also how we say
I was thinking about you
, and
are you all right
, and
come inside
, and
sit down
, and
there is still some in the pot
.
There is no way to translate it that does not come out sounding like love.
